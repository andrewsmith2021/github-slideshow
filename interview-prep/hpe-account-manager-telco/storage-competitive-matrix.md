# Storage Competitive Matrix: HPE vs. NetApp vs. Pure Storage (Everpure)

Note: this matrix compares storage products specifically (HPE, NetApp, Pure/Everpure). **Dell** is also a major infra competitor overall (see company-and-role.md section 3) — its as-a-service consumption model, **APEX**, is covered separately below since it's structurally a close parallel to GreenLake rather than a storage-product-tier matchup.

One thing to know before any of this comes up: **Pure Storage renamed itself to "Everpure, Inc." in February 2026** (ticker still PSTG). Nothing about the products, SKUs, contracts, or support changed — it's a corporate rebrand to be seen as a "data platform" company rather than a storage-only vendor, aimed at winning conversations with AI/data buyers rather than infrastructure buyers. Products are transitioning to Everpure naming through 2026, so you'll see both names in the wild for a while. Knowing this cold is a good "I'm current" flex if storage competitors come up.

## Disaggregated vs. converged: how NetApp actually responds

HPE's Alletra Storage MP (including the X10000) is a purpose-built **disaggregated** architecture — compute and storage nodes scale independently — reportedly influenced by the same "Disaggregated Shared Everything" design pattern VAST Data pioneered. NetApp's mainline ASA/AFF products (including the A20) run **ONTAP**, a converged architecture where compute and storage sit together in the same controller. NetApp's actual competitive response to a disaggregated pitch isn't one argument, it's two:

1. **Defend the converged/proven story.** ONTAP/WAFL has ~20+ years of production-hardened reliability data, all accumulated on a converged design. NetApp argues disaggregation introduces failure modes that don't exist in converged systems — network partitions between compute and storage tiers, added latency for metadata operations that used to happen locally, more complex failure-domain management — and can frame a newer disaggregated platform (HPE's MP launched Nov 2024) as an early-adopter risk for mission-critical infrastructure. Their ASA r2 line also offers **federated clustering** (multiple converged appliances aggregating into one logical system) as a scale-out answer that avoids those specific new failure modes.
2. **Meet disaggregation head-on rather than cede the segment.** NetApp announced **AFX** at Insight 2025 (Oct 2025) — a genuinely disaggregated architecture aimed at AI/exascale workloads, pitched as "parallel-file-system scalability without the complexity." Their differentiation claim is continuity: one ONTAP software stack (same data services, same operational model) across converged A-series boxes and disaggregated AFX alike. Some analysts have pushed back on this as a stretch — WAFL was designed assuming local storage access, so retrofitting disaggregation onto it is architecturally different from HPE's clean-sheet disaggregated design.

**If this comes up (interview or a real deal):** don't get defensive about disaggregation being "new." Counter with third-party validation, not just architecture claims — Alletra Storage MP X10000's NVIDIA certification, HPE's Gartner Leader placement, and GreenLake's SLA-backed terms (which transfer operational risk to HPE rather than leaving the customer to de-risk a new architecture alone). There's also a direct third-party comparison worth knowing exists: **DCIG published a head-to-head report on HPE Alletra Storage MP B10000 vs. NetApp ASA R2** — reference it if a real bake-off ever comes up, even without having read the full thing.

## GreenLake vs. Dell APEX — same layered architecture, so it's not a standalone edge

Worth knowing precisely: **Dell APEX is architecturally almost a mirror of GreenLake.** Don't lean on "we have an as-a-service model and Dell doesn't" — it isn't true, and it'll fall apart if the customer knows APEX at all.

| Layer | HPE | Dell |
|---|---|---|
| Platform/portal/billing | GreenLake | **APEX Console** — cloud-hosted platform for procurement, deployment, health monitoring |
| Storage orchestration | Data Services Cloud Console (DSCC) | **APEX Navigator** — unifies storage management/deployment across environments, then launches into product-specific managers |
| Storage control plane | (per-product, via DSCC) | **PowerStore Manager**, **PowerFlex Manager** |
| Compute control plane | Compute Ops Management | **OpenManage / iDRAC** |
| Networking control plane | Aruba Central / Juniper Mist | **SmartFabric / Dell Networking** |

**Consumption economics are nearly identical too**: Dell APEX uses committed baseline capacity (billed whether consumed or not, 1-5 year terms) plus buffer/burst capacity (instantly available, no hardware-deployment wait, billed monthly on actual use) — the same structure as GreenLake's committed-baseline-plus-burst model. One specific Dell mechanic worth knowing: metered billing for storage/memory products is capped at 85% of total installed capacity, giving customers a hard ceiling on monthly spend.

**Where the real gap is — telco/service-provider edge AI specifically.** There's no confirmed Dell equivalent to HPE's AI Grid — the specific "orchestrate thousands of distributed inference sites as one system" pitch aimed at service providers/telcos (with TELUS and CityFibre also named as interested service providers alongside Comcast). Dell's AI narrative currently centers on raw rack-scale hardware volume (Dell claims 2x+ the rack-scale AI server shipments of its closest competitor, plus the newer PowerRack line) — a manufacturing-scale/hyperscaler-and-enterprise-data-center argument, not a distributed-edge-for-carriers one. That's the real, defensible edge to claim in front of Comcast — not "GreenLake exists and APEX doesn't."

## Pure/Everpure's edge-architecture play — a real, coherent counter-story

Everpure isn't a theoretical competitor for edge/distributed AI architectures — they're actively pitching telcos on this directly. At **MWC Barcelona 2026**, Everpure targeted telecom leaders with a platform pitch for "critical workloads (CNFs, analytics, and AI) with consistent, predictable performance and automation **from edge to core to cloud**" — the same event and audience HPE used for its own service-provider AI Grid messaging.

Their toolkit, piece by piece:

1. **Portworx** — software-defined, Kubernetes-native storage that runs directly on whatever compute is already at a distributed site, rather than shipping a full storage appliance everywhere. Conceptually similar to HPE's Data Fabric-on-ProLiant-edge-nodes approach, but purpose-built for containerized/cloud-native workloads specifically — arguably a stronger fit for containerized inference agents (exactly what's running in Comcast's field trial) than a general-purpose data fabric.
2. **Pure1 Edge Platform** — their direct answer to "manage thousands of distributed sites as one system." Lightweight agents form a thin communication layer between Pure1 Cloud and on-prem arrays, allowing only specific, pre-approved tasks to execute remotely — task coordination and observability across many distributed systems from one console. A legitimate parallel to what GreenLake (via Compute Ops Management/DSCC) does for HPE's AI Grid footprint, with a specific security-model detail (approved-task-only agents) worth knowing.
3. **The 1touch acquisition** — data discovery, classification, and contextualization "across all datasets and any environment — from SaaS to the edge," making data AI-ready at the source. Direct counter to the Alletra X10000 Data Intelligence Node's in-line metadata/vector enrichment story, and reinforces Everpure's post-rebrand "data platform, not just storage" positioning.
4. **NVIDIA validation targeted at inference specifically** — OVX-ready reference architecture, BasePod certification, and a **Key Value Accelerator** (high-performance caching to accelerate AI inference in multi-GPU environments, with planned NVIDIA Dynamo integration). This targets the exact workload class Comcast's trial is testing — real-time edge inference — not just bulk AI training infrastructure.
5. **Physical footprint/power efficiency** — FlashBlade's density and lower power consumption is an explicit efficiency claim, potentially relevant if a given regional facility is power/space-constrained (worth checking against Alletra MP's chassis footprint — see technical-specs-appendix.md — since the MP chassis is a full 33"-deep 2U unit per node).
6. **Evergreen//One** — same SLA-driven consumption model already covered above, competing directly with GreenLake's economics for edge deployments specifically.

**Where HPE still has the structural edge:** Everpure has no equivalent to ProLiant or Juniper — they're a storage/data company, not a full-stack vendor. A Pure-based edge architecture needs a separate compute and networking partner, reintroducing multi-vendor integration friction that matters *more*, not less, at distributed-edge scale (thousands of sites means thousands of places for integration seams to fail). HPE also has the existing, named, live relationship — ProLiant is already confirmed running in Comcast's actual field trial; there's no comparably public Comcast (or major cable operator) trial found for Everpure.

**How to use this:** don't dismiss Pure's edge story as weak if it comes up — Portworx + Pure1 Edge + 1touch is a real, coherent answer to the same distributed-management problem GreenLake solves. The honest, defensible differentiator is full-stack integration and the head start of an existing Comcast relationship, not "Pure can't do edge."

## How to use this in the interview

- **If asked "who competes with HPE storage"**, answer with the segment, not a vendor list: NetApp and Pure/Everpure compete across nearly the entire Alletra range, but the fight looks different depending on tier — entry/capacity deals often come down to price and consumption model (GreenLake vs. Keystone vs. Evergreen), while AI/high-end deals come down to NVIDIA-certification story and the "unified platform" narrative.
- **HPE's strongest wedge vs. both**: GreenLake's as-a-service economics combined with a genuinely single hardware platform (Alletra Storage MP) spanning block and object/file — NetApp's unified story is software-only (ONTAP) across separate hardware tiers, and Pure only recently extended object support to FlashArray.
- **HPE's weakest spot vs. Pure specifically**: Pure's Evergreen//Forever non-disruptive-upgrade model is a well-known, trusted differentiator (no forklift upgrades) that predates GreenLake and has strong customer loyalty — don't dismiss it if a Pure incumbent account brings it up.
- **HPE's weakest spot vs. NetApp specifically**: ONTAP's data-management maturity (SnapMirror replication, broad enterprise track record) and now DGX SuperPOD certification on AFF A90 — NetApp isn't just an entry-tier player anymore.
- Comcast context: if storage comes up for the AI Grid / edge-AI conversation (see comcast-account.md), the relevant HPE product is **Alletra Storage MP X10000** given its NVIDIA certification and role feeding AI pipelines — position it alongside ProLiant and Juniper as one integrated stack, not a separate storage sale.
